# Training guidance for Kay

Status: DRAFT for Kay's review, 2026-10-26.
Built from (private repo, experts/): nippard.md, galpin.md, sims.md,
colenso-semple.md, holmes.md, all dated 2026-10-26. Plus the reviews and
meta-analyses listed under Evidence.

The only training principles coach-training uses. Each rule names its sources. Where
experts disagreed, a "Resolved" line says what won and why; the model doesn't
re-decide it. If something isn't covered here, say so instead of improvising.

Out of scope on purpose: the menstrual cycle, body weight, waist, and calorie targets
(MacroFactor's job), nutrition (the nutrition guidance), race training.

## Kay's context
- Advanced lifter (2+ years), plateaued. Goal: more muscle, look more toned, gain
  slowly.
- About 4 lifting days a week, sessions around 60 minutes.
- Gym: machines, cables, and dumbbells; no barbell. Almost every exercise is a machine
  or cable movement.
- Cardio is general conditioning. Daily walking and post-meal walks continue every day.

## Volume
- Aim for about 10 to 20 hard sets per muscle per week. More sets keep adding growth,
  with diminishing returns per set. (Galpin, Nippard; evidence: Pelland 2024)
- Count sets the same way every time: a set that directly targets the muscle counts 1;
  a set where it works as a helper counts 0.5 (e.g. triceps on a chest press, biceps on
  a row, glutes on a leg press). (Pelland 2024)
- Below about 10 for a muscle Kay wants to grow is a gap to fix at the next block, not
  mid-block.

## Frequency
- Train each muscle about twice a week. (Nippard, Galpin, Colenso-Semple)
- Resolved: when weekly sets are equal, frequency on its own adds little. Twice a week
  matters because it's the practical way to reach 10 to 20 sets inside ~60-minute
  sessions without junk sets at the end of a long session. (Pelland 2024)
- Leave about 48 to 72 hours before training the same muscle hard again. (Galpin)

## Effort
- Most working sets end 1 to 2 reps short of failure. (Nippard; evidence: Refalo 2023,
  which found failure not clearly better than near-failure)
- The last set of an exercise may go to failure on machines and cables. Leg press and
  hip hinges (RDL, back extension) stop 1 to 2 short. (Nippard)

## Reps
- About 6 to 12 reps for most exercises; muscle grows across roughly 5 to 30 if sets
  are close to failure. Isolation and small muscles do well at the higher end.
  (Colenso-Semple, Galpin)
- Keep range of motion and tempo the same week to week so progress is real: lift the
  hard part with intent, control the lowering, no deliberate slow-motion reps.
  (Colenso-Semple, Nippard)

## Lengthened partials
- Exercises marked (LP): after the last set reaches failure, continue with partial
  reps in the stretched half until no more are possible. Last set only. (Nippard;
  evidence: Wolf 2023, a tentative benefit at long muscle lengths)
- Resolved: Colenso-Semple notes partials make progress hard to track; stopping at the
  same stretched point each week fixes that, and keeping them to the last set limits
  the recovery cost. (Nippard)

## Rest between sets
- Rest at least about 90 seconds. Around 2 minutes on the first compound of a session,
  about 3 on heavy lower-body work (leg press, hip thrust, RDL). Isolation can stay
  near 90 seconds. Starting the next set when ready is fine. (Colenso-Semple, Nippard;
  evidence: Singer 2024)
- Resolved: the experts favor 2 to 3 minutes, but the evidence shows clear benefit
  only beyond about 60 seconds and mixed results above that. Rest long enough that the
  next set isn't worse, within the 60-minute session.

## Progression
- Double progression: keep the weight until every set reaches the top of the rep
  range, then add the smallest available increment and work back up. (Nippard,
  Galpin)
- Progressive overload and consistency drive results more than any single variable.
  (Galpin)
- Same exercise, same setup, same endpoint each week, so a number change means real
  progress. (Nippard, Colenso-Semple)
- Before calling a stall or a trend, name the window and the number of sessions. Under
  3 data points, it's too early to say. (Kay's rule, from Google's Personal Health
  Agent paper)

## Blocks and deloads
- Train in blocks with a lighter week after (about 8 weeks, then a deload). (Nippard,
  Galpin) [Nippard's deload specifics unverified]
- Program changes (exercises, split, volume) happen between blocks, not mid-block, so
  progress within a block stays comparable.

## Cardio and lifting in one week
- Normal amounts of cardio don't reduce muscle growth or maximal strength; interference
  shows mostly in explosive strength, and only when cardio and lifting are within about
  20 minutes of each other. Separated by 3 hours or more, no effect showed. Running and
  cycling didn't differ. (Galpin; evidence: Schumann 2022)
- Resolved: mostly easy cardio plus one interval session a week. Sims favors hard
  intervals over moderate work; Galpin favors mostly easy work plus some intervals;
  Colenso-Semple says structured cardio is optional for active people. Kay's choice
  combines them.
- Default week: about 2 easy sessions of ~30 minutes (talk-test pace: bike, elliptical,
  incline walk, or an easy run) plus 1 interval session (e.g. several 1 to 4 minute
  efforts, or short 30-second sprints). This is a starting point Kay can change.
- Placement: never before lifting in the same session; separate from lifting by 3
  hours or more where possible; no hard intervals or long cardio the day before or the
  morning of a leg day; prefer easy cardio after upper days or on rest days.
  (Colenso-Semple, Galpin; evidence: Schumann 2022)
- Plan lifting and cardio together, as one week. A long or hard cardio session is
  training load: the same day doesn't also get another lifting session.

## Recovery and sleep
- Mild soreness doesn't mean skip; soreness that causes missed sessions costs more
  volume than it's worth, so err toward less. (Galpin)
- A consistent bedtime and wake time is the base habit for recovery. (Holmes)
  [sources thin, unverified]
- After a clearly bad night: keep the session, trim volume (drop a set per exercise),
  and don't chase personal records that day. (Holmes, Galpin)

## Evidence
- Pelland JC et al. 2024. The resistance training dose-response: meta-regressions
  exploring the effects of weekly volume and frequency on muscle hypertrophy and
  strength gain. SportRxiv preprint (not peer reviewed):
  https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908
- Refalo MC et al. 2023. Influence of resistance training proximity-to-failure on
  skeletal muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review with meta-analysis. Sports Medicine:
  https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Influence_of_Resistance_Training_Proximity-to-Failure_on_Skeletal_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-analysis/22030796
- Singer A et al. 2024. Give it a rest: a systematic review with Bayesian
  meta-analysis on the effect of inter-set rest interval duration on muscle
  hypertrophy. Frontiers in Sports and Active Living:
  https://doaj.org/article/18363f4031e0487793db2084bee23f38
- Schumann M et al. 2022. Compatibility of concurrent aerobic and strength training
  for skeletal muscle size and function: an updated systematic review and
  meta-analysis. Sports Medicine 52:601-612.
- Wolf M et al. 2023. Partial vs full range of motion resistance training: a
  systematic review and meta-analysis. International Journal of Strength and
  Conditioning: https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182
